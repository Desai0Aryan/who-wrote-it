The stock market is a system where people buy and sell ownership shares (stocks) in publicly traded companies.

**Why companies sell shares:** When a company wants to raise money, it can "go public" through an initial public offering (IPO), selling slices of ownership to investors. In return, the company gets capital to grow, and investors get a claim on part of its future profits.

**Where trading happens:** Shares are traded on exchanges like the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq. These are organized marketplaces that match buyers with sellers. Most people don't trade directly; they use a brokerage that submits orders on their behalf.

**How prices are set:** Prices come from supply and demand. If more people want to buy a stock than sell it, the price rises; if more want to sell, it falls. Each trade happens at a price where a buyer and seller agree. Prices move based on company earnings, economic data, interest rates, news, and investor sentiment about the future.

**How investors make money:**
- **Price appreciation:** buying a share and selling it later at a higher price.
- **Dividends:** some companies distribute a portion of profits to shareholders regularly.

**Key concepts:**
- **Market cap:** a company's total value (share price × number of shares).
- **Indexes:** benchmarks like the S&P 500 or Dow Jones that track groups of stocks to gauge overall market performance.
- **Bull vs. bear markets:** sustained rising vs. falling prices.
- **Diversification:** spreading money across many investments to reduce risk, often done through index funds or ETFs.

**Risk:** Stock prices can fall as well as rise, and you can lose money. Historically, the broad market has trended upward over long periods, but with significant short-term swings.

I'm not a financial advisor, so for decisions about your own investments, it's worth consulting one.

```python
def merge_sorted(a, b):
    """Merge two sorted lists into one sorted list in O(n + m) time."""
    result = []
    i = j = 0

    while i < len(a) and j < len(b):
        if a[i] <= b[j]:
            result.append(a[i])
            i += 1
        else:
            result.append(b[j])
            j += 1

    # Append whatever remains in either list
    result.extend(a[i:])
    result.extend(b[j:])
    return result


# Example
print(merge_sorted([1, 3, 5, 7], [2, 4, 6, 8, 10]))
# [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10]
```

It walks through both lists with two pointers, always taking the smaller current element, then appends the leftover tail of whichever list isn't exhausted. Using `<=` keeps the merge stable. (Python's standard library also offers `heapq.merge(a, b)`, which does this lazily.)
