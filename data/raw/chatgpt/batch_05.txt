 The **Electoral College** is the process the United States uses to formally elect its president and vice president. It is not a physical place; it is a system established by the Constitution.

 Here is the basic process:

 1. **Each state gets electoral votes.**\
    There are currently **538 electoral votes**. A state's total equals the number of its members of Congress: its two senators plus its representatives in the House. Washington, D.C., receives 3 electoral votes.  National Archives+1
2. **Voters cast ballots in November.**\
    When Americans vote for president, they are technically voting for a slate of electors pledged to a particular presidential candidate. In most states, the candidate who wins the state's popular vote receives all of that state's electoral votes. Maine and Nebraska use a different system that can divide their electoral votes.  National Archives+1
3. **The electors cast their votes.**\
    In December, the appointed electors meet in their respective states and cast separate votes for president and vice president.  National Archives
4. **Congress counts the votes.**\
    Congress meets in January to formally count the electoral votes. A candidate needs **270 of the 538 electoral votes** to win the presidency.  National Archives+1
5. **If nobody gets 270 votes, Congress decides.**\
    Under the 12th Amendment, the House of Representatives chooses the president from the three presidential candidates who received the most electoral votes. Each state delegation gets one vote. The Senate chooses the vice president from the top two vice-presidential electoral vote recipients.  National Archives

 One important consequence is that the Electoral College result and the **national popular vote** can differ, because presidential elections are determined by electoral votes rather than by adding up votes nationwide.  National Archives

 === ANSWER 3 ===

```
class Queue:
    def __init__(self):
        self.in_stack = []
        self.out_stack = []

    def enqueue(self, item):
        """Add an item to the back of the queue."""
        self.in_stack.append(item)

    def dequeue(self):
        """Remove and return the item at the front."""
        if not self.out_stack:
            # Move items only when necessary.
            while self.in_stack:
                self.out_stack.append(self.in_stack.pop())

        if not self.out_stack:
            raise IndexError("dequeue from empty queue")

        return self.out_stack.pop()

    def peek(self):
        """Return the front item without removing it."""
        if not self.out_stack:
            while self.in_stack:
                self.out_stack.append(self.in_stack.pop())

        if not self.out_stack:
            raise IndexError("peek from empty queue")

        return self.out_stack[-1]

    def is_empty(self):
        return not self.in_stack and not self.out_stack
```

 The idea is that `in_stack` receives new elements, while `out_stack` supplies elements in queue order. When `out_stack` is empty, the elements are transferred from `in_stack`, reversing their order and putting the oldest element on top.

 `enqueue()` is **O(1)**. `dequeue()` and `peek()` are **O(1) amortized**, because each element is transferred between the stacks at most once before being removed.

 === ANSWER 5 ===

```
find . -type f -printf '%s %p\n' | sort -nr | head -n 10
```

 This:

 - `find . -type f` finds regular files recursively from the current directory.
- `-printf '%s %p\n'` prints each file's size in bytes followed by its path.
- `sort -nr` sorts numerically (`-n`) in reverse order (`-r`), putting the largest files first.
- `head -n 10` keeps the first 10 results.

 If you want **human-readable sizes**, a slightly more elaborate version using `du` can be used, but the command above is convenient because it sorts by exact byte size.

 The Fox and the Crow

A fox once saw a crow sitting high in a tree with a piece of cheese in her beak.

 “What a magnificent bird!” the fox called. “Your feathers are beautiful, but surely your voice must be even more wonderful. Would you sing for me?”

 The crow, flattered by the praise, opened her beak to sing. The cheese fell straight to the ground, where the fox quickly snatched it.

 “Thank you,” said the fox. “Remember: praise is sweetest when it costs nothing to the person giving it.”

 The crow flew away, wiser and quieter than before.

 **Moral: Beware of praise that is meant to make you forget your judgment.**
